Exosome therapy has moved quickly from research laboratories into clinics, skincare counters and social media feeds.

With that speed comes a reasonable question: is it safe?

The honest answer is that it depends. Not on exosomes as a single idea, but on what is actually in the product, how it was made, how it is given and who is giving it.

In [an earlier article](/journal/exosomes-cell-communication-and-gene-activity) we explored what exosomes are and why scientists find them interesting. Here we look at the practical side.

## What Is Exosome Therapy?

Exosomes are tiny vesicles that cells release to carry proteins, lipids and RNA to other cells. They are part of a broader family called extracellular vesicles.

Exosome therapy is a general term for using these vesicles, usually collected from cells grown in a laboratory, with the aim of influencing how the body's own cells behave.

Unlike stem cell therapy, exosome therapy does not involve giving living cells. That is one reason researchers are interested in it. But "cell-free" does not automatically mean risk-free.

## What Does the Research Say About Safety?

The research is encouraging in places, and incomplete in others.

A 2024 systematic review analysed 21 clinical reports of extracellular-vesicle-based therapies in humans. The authors reported low rates of serious adverse events across the studies they examined, and described the approach as promising.

They were also clear about the limits. Products were manufactured in very different ways, studies were designed differently, and adverse events were not reported consistently. They called for better standardisation before firm conclusions can be drawn.

<p class="study">Read the study: <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/jev2.12458" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">A systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical trials assessing safety and efficacy of human extracellular vesicle-based therapy</a></p>

Real-world use outside research settings tells a more cautionary story. In 2019, the US Food and Drug Administration reported serious adverse events in patients who had received unapproved products marketed as containing exosomes, and noted that it had not approved any exosome products.

<p class="study">Read the notice: <a href="https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/safety-availability-biologics/public-safety-notification-exosome-products" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">US FDA: Public Safety Notification on Exosome Products</a></p>

The difference between these two pictures matters. Carefully made products studied under controlled conditions are not the same as products sold with little oversight.

## Why the Product Matters

Two products both labelled "exosomes" can be very different. Important questions include:

- **Source.** Which type of cell were the vesicles collected from?
- **Manufacturing.** How were they separated, purified and tested for contamination?
- **Characterisation.** Has the product been tested to confirm what it actually contains?

The International Society for Extracellular Vesicles publishes guidance on how extracellular vesicles should be separated and characterised in research. It is a useful reminder of how much careful work goes into knowing what a sample contains.

<p class="study">Read the guidelines: <a href="https://doi.org/10.1002/jev2.12404" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Minimal information for studies of extracellular vesicles (MISEV2023)</a></p>

If a provider cannot tell you where their product comes from or how it was tested, that uncertainty is part of the risk.

## IV, Injected or Topical: Why the Route Matters

Exosome products are offered in several ways, and each carries a different level of risk.

**Topical use**, such as serums applied to the skin after a facial procedure, keeps the product on or near the surface of the body.

**Injection** into a specific area places the product inside tissue, so sterility and quality become more important.

**Exosome IV therapy**, where the product is given into a vein, sends it through the bloodstream to the whole body. This route calls for the highest standards of product quality, medical assessment and monitoring.

The same product is not equally suitable for every route. A doctor should be able to explain why a particular route has been chosen for you.

## Exosome Therapy in Malaysia

Exosome therapy is now offered by a growing number of clinics in Malaysia, including in Kuala Lumpur and Petaling Jaya.

Products intended as medical treatments would generally be expected to fall under the National Pharmaceutical Regulatory Agency's framework for biological products. Skincare products listing exosome-derived ingredients may instead be notified as cosmetics, which involves a much lighter level of review than a medicine.

Private clinics must also be licensed under the Private Healthcare Facilities and Services Act 1998, and doctors must be registered with the Malaysian Medical Council.

If a provider cannot tell you which category their product falls into, that is worth noticing.

## Questions to Ask Before Exosome Therapy

1. **What exactly is the product?** Ask for the source, the manufacturer and how it was tested.
2. **What is its regulatory status in Malaysia?** Is it registered for this use, or offered within a clinical trial?
3. **Why this route?** If IV is recommended, ask why, and how you will be monitored.
4. **Who is assessing you?** A registered doctor should review your health history before any treatment.
5. **What evidence supports this specific use?** Look for published research in people, not only laboratory work or testimonials.
6. **What are the risks, and what happens if something goes wrong?** Ask how follow-up works and who to contact.
